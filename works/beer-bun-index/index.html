The Beer-Bun Index

How bakery and beer-place density were measured across 500 m hexagons in five German cities, and what the resulting maps actually show when you compare them city by city.

Methodology

Each city is covered by a regular grid of 500 m pointy-top hexagons, built in EPSG:3035 (LAEA Europe) and clipped to the city's administrative boundary. The pipeline runs the same seven stages for every city — nothing here is Berlin-specific or hand-tuned per place.

01
Boundary. Pulled from Overture's division_area theme. Berlin and Hamburg are city-states, so their whole-city polygon is the region subtype; München, Frankfurt, and Köln use the county (kreisfreie Stadt) subtype instead, since Overture's locality layer is fragmented into individual districts for those three. Offshore exclaves (e.g. Hamburg's Neuwerk island) are dropped, keeping only the main landmass.
02
Hex grid. A 500 m grid is generated over each boundary and clipped to it.
03
Population. Germany's nationwide Zensus 2022 100 m population grid is spatially joined into each hex and summed.
04
Places. Bakeries and beer/pub places are queried from Overture Maps by category keyword, clipped to the city boundary, then deduplicated (name similarity + within 15 m) since a single real bakery often appears as 2–3 near-duplicate records.
05
Assignment. Each cleaned place is assigned to the one hex containing it.
06
Smoothing. Population and place counts are each averaged with the hex's own value plus its up-to-6 neighbors, before the rate is computed — see below.
07
Rate. Places per 1,000 residents, computed from the smoothed counts.

Why smoothing

A raw per-hex rate is noisy at 500 m: a hex with zero bakeries sitting next to a hex with five looks artificially empty, even though residents there are a five-minute walk from bakeries. Every rate in this report is therefore computed from neighbor-smoothed counts, not raw per-hex counts.

rate(hex) = 1000 × mean(count, count of ≤6 neighbors) / mean(population, population of ≤6 neighbors)

Hexes clipped at the city boundary have fewer than 6 neighbors; the mean is taken over however many exist. Population and count are smoothed independently, then divided — this avoids compounding the smoothing into the ratio itself.

What "beer place" means here

Overture's categories.primary values bar, pub, cocktail_bar, beer_garden, gastropub, sports_bar, beer_bar, gay_bar, and hotel_bar are all counted — a broad "somewhere to get a drink" definition, not narrowly craft-beer bars. "Bakery" is Overture's bakery category plus a few keyword-matched pastry/pretzel variants.

Frankfurt has the sparsest bakery provision (0.448/1k) but the highest beer-place rate (0.809/1k) of any city — the widest beer-over-bakery skew (1.80×) alongside Berlin. Köln has the densest bakery provision (0.518/1k) of the five. Hamburg sits at the other extreme on drinking establishments: it has the lowest beer rate (0.622/1k) and the narrowest beer-to-bakery gap (1.40×) — proportionally, Hamburg is the closest of the five cities to "one beer place per bakery."

3

City by city

Per-city detail: place counts, city-wide rates, and how concentrated supply is — the share of every bakery or beer place that sits inside just the busiest 10% of populated hexes. A high share means the index is being driven by a small cluster of hyper-dense blocks (a market square, a nightlife strip) rather than being spread evenly across the city.

4

Comparing the five

Concentration: how much of the supply sits in the busiest 10% of hexes

City Bakery top-10% share Beer top-10% share Hexes with neither

"Hexes with neither" = share of populated hexes with zero bakeries and zero beer places, after smoothing — i.e. purely residential blocks with no walkable bakery or bar in the immediate hex.

Hamburg is the most spatially concentrated city on both metrics at once: 93.6% of its bakeries and 90.4% of its beer places sit in the busiest tenth of populated hexes, and 81.8% of populated hexes have neither. Combined with the lowest city-wide beer rate, this points to a city where supply is unusually clustered into a few dense nodes (its historic core and nightlife strips) against a much larger residential footprint with none at all — consistent with Hamburg's hex count and area being the largest after Berlin, for a population smaller than Köln's.

München sits at the opposite pole: it has the lowest concentration on the bakery side (72.3%) and the lowest "neither" share (67.1%) — its bakery network reaches further into ordinary residential hexes than any other city studied, even though its city-wide bakery rate (0.477/1k) is only middling. Berlin and Frankfurt track each other closely on the beer-to-bakery ratio (1.80× each) despite Frankfurt being roughly a fifth of Berlin's population — Frankfurt's compact, business-district-heavy core produces the same relative skew toward drinking establishments as Berlin's much larger and more spread-out footprint.

Reading the median rate

The "median smoothed rate" in the city cards above is computed only over hexes that actually contain at least one bakery or beer place — it answers "when a hex has one, how strong is the local walkability signal," independent of how many hexes have zero. Köln and Hamburg post the two highest bakery medians (0.702 and 0.730) despite neither topping the city-wide bakery rate table, meaning that where a Köln or Hamburg hex does have a bakery, it tends to be part of a denser walkable cluster than the same situation in Berlin or Frankfurt.
