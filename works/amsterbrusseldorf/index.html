Can Amsterbrusseldorf Feed Itself?
Method and results · land balance

Can Amsterbrusseldorf feed itself?

The urban belt from Amsterdam through Brussels to the Ruhr is one of the most densely populated areas in Europe. We calculated whether its own land would be enough to grow what it eats.

No. At today’s diet and level of waste, the region cannot feed itself: it is short of about 4.4 million hectares. But if it did not throw away and overeat a third of its food, the balance would rise to 72 %. Below is how this was calculated and why the first figure was wrong.

01

Where the question came from

It started with a land cover map of Germany: how much of the country is fields, forest and towns. One figure came out of it: almost half of Germany is farmland, and the country’s food self-sufficiency is about 89 %.

A comment raised a fair objection: measuring food security in Europe country by country makes little sense. Cities and farming here do not follow national borders. And it named a concrete example: Amsterbrusseldorf.

Amsterbrusseldorf

An informal name for the continuous urban network between Amsterdam, Brussels and Düsseldorf. It is neither an administrative unit nor a statistical region, so it has no ready-made boundary and no statistics of its own. We had to draw the boundary ourselves.

We deliberately ask whether the region “could feed itself”, not whether it “is self-sufficient”. The difference matters.

What we calculate and what we do not

Self-sufficiency is about real flows: what is imported, exported and stored. Such data are simply not openly available at the regional level for three countries.

Capacity to feed itself is about land: how much there is and how much is needed. That is arithmetic, and it can be calculated honestly. That is what we calculate.

02

How we drew the boundary

With no official boundary, it has to be built reproducibly, so that anyone can repeat and check it. We based it on the EU statistical regions.

NUTS

The EU’s common system of statistical regions. Three nested levels: NUTS 1 is a large part of a country (for example all of North Rhine-Westphalia), NUTS 2 is a province, NUTS 3 is the finest level.

We work at NUTS 3because it is the only level with comparable granularity in all three countries: 40 COROP regions in the Netherlands, 44 arrondissements in Belgium and about 400 districts (Kreise) in Germany. At NUTS 1 we would have had to take whole federal states instead of a corridor.

Building the boundary takes four mechanical steps:

  1. Draw the corridor axis

    A polyline through cities: Amsterdam → Utrecht → Rotterdam → Antwerp → Brussels, a second branch Antwerp → Eindhoven → Venlo → Düsseldorf → Essen → Dortmund, and a third Düsseldorf → Cologne → Bonn. Three branches rather than one line, because the corridor is Y-shaped and a single polyline would draw a false diagonal across Belgium.

  2. Extend 60 km to each side

    A buffer around the axis. 60 km, because we care not only about the built-up area but also about the farmland around it that could in theory feed it.

  3. Take the regions whose centre falls inside

    The boundary follows whole NUTS 3 regions instead of cutting them in half. This matters: population is published for the whole region, and splitting it would mean dividing it by eye.

  4. Keep only the connected part

    The buffer can catch a region across a bay or a gap. A disconnected “food system region” cannot be defended, so we keep the largest continuous block.

03

Why we count hectares, not tonnes

The obvious approach is to take production in tonnes and divide it by consumption in tonnes, product by product. We rejected it, and this is the main methodological decision of the whole study. There are three reasons.

Reason one: feed

The Netherlands is a major pork exporter. But much of the feed for those pigs is soy from Brazil. Counting that pork as “local food production” is wrong: the land that grew it is in another hemisphere.

With tonnes, this gap has to be patched with a separate, more complex indicator. With hectares, the problem does not arise at all: a product’s land footprint by construction includes the land used for feed.

Reason two: incomparable statistics

The three countries have different reporting categories, levels of detail and years. Combining them is the most laborious part of such work. Land, on the other hand, comes from a satellite image that is the same on both sides of the border: the satellite does not know where Belgium ends.

Reason three: the boundary can be drawn anywhere

A raster can be cut by any polygon. That is what lets us use a functional corridor instead of a bag of ready-made statistical units, which is exactly what the original comment was about.

Raster

An image divided into a grid of identical cells (pixels), where each cell stores a value; here it is a land cover class code. Unlike vector boundaries, a raster can be clipped to any outline and summed over any area.

04

Supply: how much land there is

Sentinel-2 land cover, 10 m

A ready-made product by Esri, Impact Observatory and Microsoft: all land divided into 10×10 metre squares, each assigned to one of nine classes such as water, trees, crops and built area. It is based on 2024 imagery from the European Space Agency’s Sentinel-2 satellites. Stated accuracy is above 75 %.

The data come in tiles by grid zone. Our region falls into two: 31U and 32U. Both had already been downloaded for the earlier map of Germany, so no new downloads were needed.

A technical detail without which nothing works

One tile is 44,754 × 89,383 pixels, that is 4.0 GB uncompressed. It cannot be read into memory whole. We read it decimated: we request the reduced copy embedded in the file and take, in each coarse cell, the most frequent class. Peak read is 35 MB instead of 4 GB.

Taking the “most frequent” class is essential: class codes cannot be averaged. The average of “water” (1) and “crops” (5) would give “flooded vegetation” (4), a class that is not there.

Everything is then reprojected to EPSG:3035 on a 100-metre grid.

EPSG:3035, an equal-area projection

A way of flattening the curved surface of the Earth onto a plane. “Equal-area” means areas are not distorted: a cell in the north of the region has exactly the same area as a cell in the south. For counting hectares this is a must; in the usual Mercator projection the north would be stretched and areas overstated.

The result is a grid of 3950 × 3538 cells, 14 million pixels, clipped to the AOI boundary. Each cell is 100×100 m = 1 hectare, so measuring area comes down to counting cells.

05

An important correction: the satellite cannot tell pasture from fields

In the table above, rangeland covers only 5.2 %. For the Netherlands and Flanders, where dairy farming is the backbone of agriculture, that looked suspicious. We checked it against an independent source.

Farm Structure Survey (FSS)

The EU’s official farm census: farmers are asked directly how much land they have and what is on it. It gives land use : how land is used legally and economically. The satellite gives land cover : what is physically visible from above. These are different things, and here they diverged.

Share of permanent grassland: the two sources differ threefold
SourcePermanent grassland
Eurostat farm survey31.4 %
Esri raster, “rangeland” class9.4 %

The reason is clear if you picture a Dutch meadow: mown, fertilised, sown ryegrass. From above it looks like an ordinary cultivated field, and the algorithm puts it in the “crops” class. Esri’s “rangeland” class mostly catches semi-natural grassland.

How we solved it

The total farmland area from the raster agrees with the survey within a few percent, so it can be trusted. Only the split inside it disagrees. So: we take the total from the raster (continuous, 10 metres, recent 2024 data), and the split into arable land and grassland from the survey (authoritative on land use), separately for each NUTS 2 region.

Either source on its own would give a worse result.

06

Demand: how much land is needed

Demand is population multiplied by the land footprint of the diet: how many hectares a year it takes to feed one person.

Population

Taken directly from Eurostat for each NUTS 3 region for 2024. Because the AOI follows whole regions, the figures simply add up and nothing has to be split by eye. Total: 39,162,588 people.

What people eat

FAO Food Balance Sheets (FBS)

The UN’s annual summary for each country: how much was produced, imported, exported, used for feed and seed, and how much in the end reached people , in kilograms per person per year and kilocalories per day.

Note: this is the level of supply, meaning what reached the shop, not what was actually eaten. The difference is waste and overeating. We will come back to this; it turns out to be the most interesting part.

We use 13 non-overlapping groups: cereals, potatoes and roots, sugar, pulses, vegetable oils, vegetables, fruit, and separately beef, pork, poultry, mutton, eggs and milk.

Yields must be local

To turn kilograms into hectares you need yields. And here generic figures are completely unsuitable.

Meat and milk: feed conversion ratios

Feed conversion ratio (FCR)

How many kilograms of feed an animal must eat to produce one kilogram of product. A pig does not turn feed into meat one to one: a kilogram of pork takes several kilograms of grain. FCR converts an animal product into arable area.

A mistake we had to fix along the way

At first we used ratios per live weight of the animal. But FAO reports consumption as the product is eaten. The yield from live weight to retail is about 0.55 for pigs and 0.50 for cattle, so an edible kilogram takes almost twice as much feed.

The mistake understated the land footprint by about a third: it gave 0.12–0.14 ha per person, lower than anything published for European diets. After the fix it came to 0.15–0.18 ha, inside the known range. The mismatch with the literature was the signal that something was wrong.

Roughage is counted in dry matter , that is without water, because fresh grass is 80 % water and comparing it with grain by fresh weight makes no sense. Grassland yield is assumed at 9 t of dry matter per hectare per year.

What we checked

  • Does the area add up? The classified area matched the AOI area exactly, so clipping to the boundary works.
  • Does it match official statistics? Farmland from the raster is 3.19 million ha; the farm survey gives 3.34 million ha for regions that extend beyond the AOI. The gap is small and in the expected direction.
  • How much does the result depend on assumptions? With feed ratios changed by ±20 %, the balance lies between 44.9 % and 60.7 %. With buffers of 50, 60 and 75 km it is 45.6 %, 51.6 % and 50.1 %.

Both sensitivity checks move the figure by a few percentage points and do not change the conclusion. The problem, as it turned out, lay elsewhere.

08

Where the calculation was wrong

After getting 51.6 %, we checked three suspicions. One was confirmed in a serious way.

The model covered only 77–83 % of dietary calories

We took 13 commodity groups, but missed animal fats and butter (about 294 kcal a day for the Dutch), alcohol (129), other vegetable oils (101), as well as fish, coffee, cocoa, nuts and offal.

Demand was understated by about a fifth, which means the balance was overstated. This is the largest error in the whole study.

Along the way we found a trap: in the FAO file the rows “Eggs” and “Milk” appear twice, under different item codes, with identical values. The calculation takes the first occurrence and is therefore correct, but the very first check script added both rows and reported 102 % coverage, an impossible value that pointed to the problem.

Feed from arable land was wrongly assigned to grassland

We put all ruminant roughage on grassland. But according to Eurostat, green fodder grows mostly on arable land: 397,700 ha in the Netherlands, 276,060 ha in Belgium and 2,770,600 ha in Germany. For the Netherlands that is about 47 % of all arable land.

Silage maize yields 12–15 t of dry matter per hectare against 9 for grassland. So less land is needed for feed than we calculated. This error works the other way: it made the balance look worse.

09

The most interesting part is the question itself

The region “supplies” about 3,663 kcal per person per day. An adult actually needs around 2,200–2,500. The difference is spoilage, retail waste, food thrown away at home and overeating.

So all along we were checking whether there is enough land to provide today’s level of supply including its losses. That is a much stricter test than “is there enough land to feed everyone”.

Current supply43 %

A conclusion stronger than the original one

In terms of land, the region is almost self-sufficient, if it did not throw away and overeat a third of its food. The shortage of hectares and the shortage of thrift are of the same order here. This is closer to what food system experts actually discuss than the original question about self-sufficiency.

10

Weak points

Below is what the study does not account for. Some of it can be fixed; some needs data we do not have.

Not accounted for at all

  • The Dutch nitrogen crisis. Livestock numbers are being cut by law because of nitrogen emissions. We calculate the biophysical capacity of the land, but the real ceiling is now set by environmental regulation, not hectares. For this region it may matter more than all the other caveats.
  • Nutritional adequacy. Enough hectares does not mean a balanced diet. The region could grow the required area and still fall short on particular nutrients.
  • Energy crops. In Germany about 14 % of farmland is used for biogas and biodiesel. We treat all arable land as available for food. For a “could” question this is acceptable, but it is an unstated assumption, not a fact.
  • Greenhouses. The Westland produces 500–700 t/ha of tomatoes against the 30 t/ha we assume for vegetables, and it stands on land the satellite classifies as built-up. The region’s real vegetable capacity is higher than in the model.
  • Fish and seafood. They need no land at all and are not in the model.
  • Crop rotation, fallow, soil quality, year-to-year variation in yields.

Weak assumptions

  • Yields for vegetables (30 t/ha) and fruit (20 t/ha) are assumed, not measured : Eurostat has no regional data for these crops. Together they make up about 8 % of the land footprint.
  • Grassland yield is one constant of 9 t/ha for the whole region, although it varies noticeably.
  • Feed is calculated with local cereal yields (8.19 t/ha), whereas real compound feed is largely soy, and soy yields 2–3 t/ha. This makes the estimate optimistic.
  • The diet is a national average applied to residents by country of residence.
  • Data come from different years: land cover 2024, population 2024, yields 2022, farm survey 2020, FAO balances 2022.

Conceptual

  • This is cover, not use. The satellite sees a field but not what grows on it or who owns it.
  • This is capacity, not trade. The calculation says nothing about actual supply, imports or stocks.
  • The boundary is arbitrary in its details. The AOI also includes clearly peripheral districts such as Hochsauerland and Namur. A different set of cities on the axis would give a different figure.
  • There is no real uncertainty interval. We varied parameters one at a time; an honest confidence interval needs them varied jointly.
11

What to do next

In order of impact on the result:

  1. Raise diet coverage to about 95 % of calories

    Mechanical work, but it shifts the result the most, by 10 percentage points.

  2. Split roughage between arable land and grassland

    Using the actual green fodder areas from Eurostat instead of putting it all on grassland.

  3. Recalculate supply at 50 metres

    And make sure the figure finally converges.

  4. Replace assumed yields with real ones

    Vegetables and fruit are the only values taken out of thin air.

  5. Run a Monte Carlo analysis

    Vary feed ratios and yields jointly instead of one parameter at a time, to give an honest interval instead of a single number.

  6. Add scenarios

    “No waste” and “less meat”. These are not error fixes but the better story: they show that the demand lever is stronger here than the land lever.
